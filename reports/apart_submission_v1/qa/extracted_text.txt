 Compact Models of LLM Agent Interaction
 Predictive gains, state sufficiency, and the search for an interpretable update rule
 Research report · 14 November 2026
Abstract
Can a compact predictor capture how language-model agents influence one another, and can its behavior eventually
be expressed as an interpretable rule? We compare signed graph transformers with a reproduced Ising baseline, then
diagnose the roles of model capacity, question coverage, argument representation and stochastic responses.
Experiments use released GPT-4o-mini trajectories and a separate, newly generated Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct corpus.
On 320 training and 80 validation questions, an 818-parameter transformer achieves 84.09% next-opinion accuracy
versus 82.82% for the 19-coefficient Ising baseline. Expanding the transformer to 533,026 parameters does not
improve accuracy. Increasing training coverage from 40 to 320 questions improves accuracy by 0.29 percentage
points for the small model and 0.97 points for the largest, which remains behind. Capacity tests show that
substantially better training fit is possible but can worsen generalization. Argument features yield little consistent
unseen-question gain; however, controlled Llama message interventions demonstrate substantial text dependence at
fixed stated opinions. Simple explicit surrogates also fail to closely reproduce the compact transformer's
probabilities. These results support useful compression of conditional opinion updates, while leaving state
sufficiency, causal interpretation and reliable autonomous simulation unresolved. Most follow-up comparisons
reuse development data; a separate 40-question Llama assessment cohort remains ungenerated and unscored.
1. Introduction
Interactions among language-model agents can reinforce errors as well as improve answers. A cheap, inspectable
predictor of the next opinion could help identify when agreement is informative and when it merely reflects social
influence. The scientific question is whether a compact state admits a transferable update rule. A neural
approximation is a test of predictive compressibility; it is not, by itself, evidence for a universal or causal law.
Our starting hypothesis was that a more expressive graph transformer should predict updates better than an Ising
model. The observed gains were modest, motivating three contributions: (1) a controlled comparison across model
sizes and training-question counts, including a new 400-question Llama development corpus; (2) diagnostics
separating fitting capacity, transfer, stochastic targets and missing message information; and (3) tests of explicit
formulas, aggregate-state models, graph transfer and alternative datasets. The report consolidates the completed
studies, retaining their different evaluation roles rather than pooling their scores.
2. Related Work
Physics of Agents [1] motivates the signed-network setting and the statistical-mechanics baseline. We extend that
comparison with neural predictors and tests of what the supplied state omits; we do not replicate every result or
source model in that paper. MATH [2] supplies objective problems for the new corpus. MiniLM [3,4] supplies
frozen semantic features, and Llama 3 [5] supplies the open-weight source agents. CoevolveSim [6] and DEBATE
[7] provide separate external checks. Our focus is predicting agent responses, not solving the underlying questions
or improving the source LLM through fine-tuning.

3. Methods
Task and boundary. Each primary conversation has 32 fixed personas on a prescribed signed graph. An opinion is
the majority of five sampled A/B responses; a transition is one agent’s opinion at the next round. The principal
predictor observes current opinions, question/persona context and graph, before the next messages are generated.
Argument studies instead observe the delivered messages and predict the subsequent response. These are different
information conditions. Accuracy measures agreement with the agent’s next opinion, not mathematical correctness.

preprocessing are fitted; argument experiments additionally observe actual delivered messages.

Question selection. The Llama corpus uses rational-answer MATH problems, balanced correct A/B placement,
seven topics and eight familiar graphs. Candidates are ranked by pooled initial-response entropy from 32 personas ×
eight draws; selected questions are the top quarter within topic/answer cells. The expansion retains the original
80/20 development questions and adds 240/60 from 1,200 fresh candidates. Screening draws are separate from
conversation draws, and predictor errors do not select questions. Each question has one eight-update conversation,
giving 81,920 training and 20,480 validation transitions. Appendix A records selection and source limitations.
Predictors. The reproduced Ising implementation stores 19 fitted coefficients: 16 context coefficients and three
social-channel coefficients, not a separate coefficient for each persona. Transformers share weights across agents
and use four attention heads, a signed edge mask and one attention block. The expanded-study context contains
three persona components, three question components, nine products and a constant. MiniLM’s 22.7 million
pretrained parameters and PCA are outside the quoted neural parameter counts. Arguments are absent from the main
scaling study.
Comparison and uncertainty. At 40/80/160 questions, three balanced nested subset paths are shared across
models; the full 320-question endpoint is reused once. Question PCA is fitted on each training subset. Every
size/count receives nine learning-rate/penalty choices, validation-NLL stopping, then three fresh-seed fits at frozen
settings. Update budgets scale with data size to preserve maximum passes. We average model scores, not ensemble
predictions. Paired 95% bootstrap intervals resample questions after seed/subset averaging; they are exploratory,
pointwise and conditional on the fitted models. They do not treat agents or rounds as independent observations.

4.3 Fitting capacity is not the main demonstrated bottleneck
On the original GPT cohort, a 135,458-parameter transformer imitates Ising probabilities to 0.022 pp training MAE.
Training without validation stopping lowers observed-label training error from 8.14% to 4.41%, while validation
error rises from 10.59% to 12.22% and NLL worsens. The expanded Llama sweep likewise shows better late
training fit with worse validation loss. Similar validation-selected training errors therefore do not establish equal
representational capacity (Appendix B).

4.4 Arguments matter to the source, but current features transfer weakly
The GPT argument transformer processes individual delivered messages through a frozen encoder. Yet its
familiar-question error reduction, 6.47%→5.93%, can be reproduced by question IDs alone (5.92%). Across unseen
questions, richer question context, alternate fusion, joint question-message encoding and order features do not
establish a robust argument advantage. In the eight-question Llama pilot, arguments improve held-out accuracy only
from 70.64% to 70.90%. These results concern the tested representations and limited cohorts, not the irrelevance of
language (Appendix C).
Controlled Llama interventions give complementary evidence: replacing rationales with conclusion-only messages
changes first-token P(A) by 22.40 pp on average across 489 nonempty inboxes with explicitly matching stances;
the dominant token choice changes in 130. Thus fixed opinions, personas and graph need not determine the
full-prompt response distribution. Minimal validity edits have much smaller effects than rationale deletion, and
validity grading is near chance. These experiments establish text dependence, not reliable logical-quality
recognition. First-token probabilities and sampled five-vote opinions are different targets (Appendix D).
4.5 Transfer and interpretability remain qualified
On four fresh graphs and 20 reused questions, the 320-question transformer reaches 84.35% versus Ising’s 81.37%,
a 2.98 pp advantage (1.18–4.98). Both use the same graph family and known personas; broader graph transfer
remains untested in this Llama study. On GPT development folds, an 809-weight aggregate-state network reaches
9.57% error versus 9.12% for graph attention and 10.95% for Ising. Explicit formulas with up to 42 coefficients still
miss the compact transformer’s probabilities by about 4.9 pp. Neither result identifies a unique law (Appendices
E–F).
5. Discussion and Limitations
The evidence supports a useful conditional predictor whose gains concentrate on difficult updates. It does not show
that opinions are a sufficient state, that attention weights are causal credibility, or that one-step accuracy guarantees
reliable simulation. The original frozen GPT test found a modest one-step gain but no consistent autonomous-rollout
improvement. Larger models can overfit, and generic frozen text features may fail to preserve useful reasoning
distinctions. Noise, omitted information and limited question transfer remain separate contributors. Text
interventions do not rule out a calibrated opinion-only forecast that averages over future message generation.
Scope. The principal tasks are objective mathematics under snap-judgment prompts, prescribed friendly/unfriendly
relations and fixed personas. The new corpus has one trajectory per question and entropy selection favors
substantial within-agent randomness. Most follow-ups reuse validation questions; confidence intervals omit full
training-resampling and selection uncertainty. Original GPT test results are already consumed; the separate
40-question Llama assessment is still ungenerated. External and in-context pilots are supportive diagnostics, not
replications on the same population.
Future work. Freeze the next predictor before fresh assessment; separate breadth from transition count; collect
repeated full-prompt responses and matched message interventions on new questions; test learned text
representations and longer-horizon calibration. Learned mutual credibility, unfamiliar personas and graph families
require separately designed experiments.
6. Conclusion
A compact transformer can improve on an Ising baseline without needing a large update network. More data helps,
but greater size has not produced better average accuracy in the tested range. The strongest next step is to improve
and test the state representation while preserving independent assessment, rather than interpreting a small predictive
gap as a completed explanation of agent influence.

A.2 Baseline and signed attention
Let s be the current ±1 opinion vector, J the signed adjacency matrix, and φ the 16-component context. Define Fi =
Σj max(Jij,0)sj and Ui = Σj min(Jij,0)sj. The reproduced baseline uses logit P(A next) = w·φi + aFi + bUi + c(Fi−Ui),
fitted with the pinned author two-stage routine. It stores 16+3=19 coefficients; the social columns are linearly
dependent, so 19 is not a claim of 19 identifiable degrees of freedom. Persona dependence comes through shared
context features, not one free weight per persona.
In the transformer, non-neighbors are masked out. Each attention head learns positive/negative edge biases and
separate signed message multipliers, plus a degree-dependent aggregation scale. Receiver and sender state/context
produce queries, keys and values. These mechanisms use the prescribed relation labels; the attention weights are not
estimates of causal influence. The 818 model has hidden width 8, four heads, key width 1 per head, value width 2
and feedforward width 16.
A.3 Source generation and selection
The original development bank contains 20 objective questions, with eight graphs, four replicas, eight updates and
32 agents: 163,840 transitions. Initial controlled fits use 16/4 question separation. The 20 original test questions
were scored only after the controlled model freeze. Subsequent nested development studies do not restore freshness
to those already examined questions.
The separate Llama source uses pinned MLX weights, float32 floating computation, temperature 0.7, top-p 1 and a
256-token cap. Generation ran locally and on UCI HPC3; each new GPU allocation had to pass a five-group
numerical replay. Request identities, raw votes, messages, prompt records and hashes support resumable collection.
The completed 400-question development corpus has 102,400 valid majority transitions; one individual vote is
unparseable without invalidating its majority.
Initial selection retained 140 of 560 candidates (80 fit, 20 validation, 40 reserved). Expansion selected 300 of 1,200
new candidates, maintaining the top-quarter rule. Near-duplicate filters use number-masked families and
character-ngram similarity, not a guarantee of semantic independence. Original distractor construction used a
deterministic wrong-option fallback for 249/560 candidates; plausibility was not independently certified. Pooled
entropy is a selection property, not an estimate of stable disagreement or predictor difficulty. Evidence: E01, E10,
E13–E16.

probabilities is 15.60%. This reference includes newly generated messages unavailable at the original forecasting
boundary, and is not an attainable Bayes bound for opinion-only inputs.
A verified repeated local opinion state has source P(A) 0.66% in one round and 98.69% in another while both
predictors remain unchanged. Other text and ordering change too. Across validation, 15.06% of updates belong to
repeated connected-agent states with source majority-probability ranges above 25 pp. These observations identify
missing information without isolating logical quality.
The transformer corrects 4.75 pp of Ising errors but introduces 3.18 pp, leaving a 1.57 pp net gain. It helps stays
(9.56% vs 12.18% error), but not switches (25.91% vs 25.43%). Pooled screening entropy averages 0.994 bits, of
which 0.873 is within-agent entropy: selection mainly finds uncertain agents, not necessarily stable disagreement.
Evidence: E08, E10–E11.

Appendix E. Attempts to simplify the learned rule
E.1 Persistence, question bias and the easy majority
On the old 20-question GPT development pool, 79.63% of updates have Ising confidence at least 0.8; Ising and the
818-parameter transformer both make about 2.17% errors there. On the remaining 20.37%, error is 45.27% for Ising
and 36.28% for the transformer. Thus aggregate accuracy conceals a larger improvement on a small difficult subset,
without providing a deployable oracle for selecting correct predictions.
Adding a training-fitted own-opinion correction to the frozen Ising logit lowers outer error from 10.95% to 10.11%;
initial-response tendency features do not improve that tested linear correction. The correction represents a predictive
association, not proven causal self-influence. In a separate same-question adaptation diagnostic, offsets fitted on
other replicas lower Ising error to 9.10% and the 34,978-parameter transformer’s error to 8.45%. Those offsets use
extra same-question outcomes and are not unseen-question predictors.

Appendix G. External data and in-context prediction
G.1 External feasibility audits
DEBATE/MALLM [7] was audited before training an argument predictor. In the simple-response/no-persona
cohort, copying the supplied draft predicts all 1,440 development updates. In the critical-response/expert-persona
cohort, only two of 360 validation updates depart from that draft. Neither audited configuration passes the recorded
feasibility gate for an argument comparison, so no such model was trained. This conclusion is about those
configurations, not the whole dataset.
